Being Water – Porto Design Biennale 2023

Title Ser Água — Como fluímos e nos moldamos coletivamente / Being Water — How we flow together and shape each other; Event Porto Design Biennale 2023, Porto and Matosinhos; Dates 19.10.2023 – 03.12.2023; Principal curator Fernando Brízio; Curatorial team Ivo Poças Martins, Margarida Mendes, Mariana Pestana, Joana Pestana, Miguel Vieira Baptista, Studio Makkink & Bey; Curator of Spain (Galicia) David Barro; Curatorial assistant Constança Cardoso; Promoted by Câmara Municipal do Porto, Câmara Municipal de Matosinhos; Organised by esad—idea, Investigação em Design e Arte; Category Curating

Water is everywhere; it shapes everything, it is vital, and life would not be possible without it. It can take many forms, manifesting them simultaneously. It is a powerful lens through which we think about the world and the adaptability of design to the fast and ceaseless changes that have been taking place in “reality”, where the world we know is disintegrating and we still don’t know how the next one will look like.

Our understanding of water is generally superficial and reductive: we perceive it, above all, as a resource, an imprisoned liquid surface, separated from the remaining matter. This restricts the way we relate to it.

The water demand is expected to outstrip supply by 40%, by 2030. How can we preserve what we still don’t know quite well? In this edition of Porto Design Biennale, we propose a transdisciplinary laboratory platform dedicated to observation, reflection, creativity, and learning, which will act simultaneously in the visible and invisible, organic and inorganic, and ephemeral spectra of water.

This platform-laboratory is “hydrated” through six proposals: Promethean Beasts: Shapes of the human · Magic Reality: Living with the un/known · Bodies Of Water: Where water becomes common — vegetable matter, meat, mineral · Dynamic Landscapes: Dancing margins, borders that don’t exist · Flying Rivers: Rethinking the sources, uses, and representations of water · Affective Geologies: The living history of a recipe

From the interconnection between these six proposals, we aim to develop and present strategies that contribute to acknowledge, repair, restore and cogitate new relationships with the world. In addition to designing better and more efficient uses of water, we must create models of alternative, symbiotic cohabitation between humans and more-than-humans, a beneficial and sustainable relationality for all. Cooperative and ecological relations that embrace the various spectres of reality and water, in their various forms and places. Instead of the same words and stories, we seek to “hydrate” with new words, bring to light other narratives, talk to strangers, and sow ideas.

From the water, we wish to nurture a new lexicon that expands our relational field with the world. Learning from water reveals the complex and intricate web of interdependencies that connects us to the planet as a whole.

Promethean Beasts: Shapes of the human

Earth and water showed us, through clay, the endless possibilities of modelling forms. The plasticity of clay may have been noticed when humans trod on clayey ground and saw hollows that held water, like the concavities of rocks or of cupped hands.

The availability of clay in the landscape and the ease of modelling it probably served for the first experiments in shaping matter and for the recognition and development of the capacity for design, central to raising resilience and the chances of human survival, mutual care. In this sense, the statement by the anthropologist Margaret Mead is well known: that the first trace of civilisation would be a broken femur that had healed. A healed femur shows that someone cared for another human being, did not leave them alone, carried them, treated their wounds, kept them company and fed them. The design and making of containers to store food made sustenance possible. The lesser need to search for food left us more time to care for one another

Clay will have been seminal in the awareness of matter, the body and being, the embodiment and shaping of the human. Perhaps that is why, in the origin myths of some religions, clay is the matter used by the gods to mould beings, and there are even societies that use the same word for clay and flesh.

Prometheus is one of those gods who, according to Greek mythology, moulded humans from clay. Disobeying Zeus, he stole fire from the gods to give it to humans, ensuring their superiority over the other animals. Prometheus gave humans thought, techniques and the arts, the possibility of progress, in short, civilisation, moving us away from nature, from the place of the wild, of the beasts. In this process the capacity for conception (design) plays a central role, making progress and culture possible. Design understood as a verb, i.e., the human aptitude that expands its capacities. “Designers (...) are choreographers of our everyday life (…) they organise points, lines and surfaces, (…) they form a relational structure within which we live, which conditions (...) movements and actions that we perform every day. Over the course of our lives this mould invisibly shapes (...) our body, our mind and the way we relate to the world. The work of design is a work that configures man” (Brízio, 2007).

Design is a process of autopoiesis, that is, of self-formation, of construction of the human. “The work of design is also a work of design of man.” (Brízio, 2011). This understanding holds a relational idea in which objects do not exist separate from us, as if inert beside us; on the contrary, they intertwine with us and transform us, just as we transform them. Objects act in the world and on us; they have a generative power as they move through human lives. They are co-responsible for the way we are human. Things are embodied; they are prolongations, physical and cognitive extensions of the body-mind. Progress based on economic growth and on the concentration of wealth, competition and individualism is destabilising and exhausting natural systems, putting everyone's future at risk. We have become creative beasts (or, to use the terminology of the cognitive zoologist Ivo Jacobs, “Promethean beasts”).

However, some indigenous peoples do not aspire to progress as this concept is understood in the West. For them the sense of unity between humans, between humans and more-than-humans, and among more-than-humans prevails over the individual. It is important to pay attention to, show and learn from other forms of the human, to seek alternatives to the anthropocentric, rational Western model, thus promoting respect and care for the whole. To look at diverse worldviews in geographies far from or close to us. To reflect on identity politics and on the metamorphosis of the human that is taking place, provoked by the endless layers of design, namely those produced by the unstoppable digitalisation of the world, which act as accelerators of radical change.

Magical Reality: Living with the un/known

Water always escapes us; we cannot grasp it. There are many unanswered questions about it or, depending on the point of view, several answers to the same question. Water is a good ‘moisturiser’ for bringing out the magical reality in which we live. At what moment does water stop being water and become plant matter, flesh? Are we inside or outside water? Does it occupy space or is it a space? Water cannot be thought of as one thing; it is many different things at the same time: ice, rain, blood, sap, ocean, cloud, vapour, sweat, mud, humidity... its form, composition and density are always changing; sometimes it transforms unexpectedly. Apparently docile, it adapts to the places and surfaces it passes through, but it also acts on them and shapes them: earth, rocks, rivers, oceans, plant matter, the heart. Fluid, water penetrates solid matter and mixes with the air; it is never just water; always in motion, it is an absorbent and a solvent, it always contains something. It only becomes empty when purified. It is a dissident material; it shows a significant number of anomalous behaviours that contradict common sense and the laws of physics, such as freezing faster when it is hot rather than cold, or not freezing at a temperature of −50 °C. Water shows us a reality that is not mechanical and linear, mysterious, full of chance and surprises, indecipherable. Water is a reality full of blind spots that challenges the very notion of the real, constantly placing us before uncertainties. It presents us with an infinity of possibilities, between fact and fiction.

There is in us a feeling of incompleteness that makes us fabulate the real, create concepts that generate and organise ideas, that comfort, resolve impasses, give meaning to life. We create fictions, worlds to come that nourish our existence and expand our understanding of the world. In this process, as Timothy Morton argues in Realist Magic: Objects, Ontology, Causality (Ann Arbor: Open Humanities Press, MPublishing – University of Michigan Library, 2013), objects are key actors, attributing symbolic power and capacities, sometimes magical, to those who own them. The example of transitional or mediating objects, which may be ordinary everyday objects, is paradigmatic. They can be existential maps, keepers of stories, activators and mediators of memories; they can carry desires, protect and console, be empowering, transforming and much more. They help us deal with experiences of separation and traumatic episodes of loss. Objects occupy a central place in the emotional infrastructure, helping us deal with memory, disorder, commotion, awe, the unknown, fear, love… the intangible. They can become powerful autobiographical anchors, emotional and intellectual companions in our everyday life, where they take on numerous roles.

Bodies of Water: Where water becomes common — plant matter, flesh and mineral

A human body is 70% water. In our existential entanglement with the material world, water is fertile and omnipresent. Life emerged in water and is not possible without it. Water flows through bodies and geographies, through the earth, between flesh and plant matter, perspiration and cloud, breath and wind. Together we shape one another, organic and inorganic, humans and more-than-humans connected through a common water flow – we share the same water that enters and exudes from bodies, and that through biology becomes other matter.

We are aqueous bodies, porous, hybrid and multiple beings. From this porosity, I think of a collective body in cooperation, coexistence, in empathy and sharing, in preservation and healing, hydration, in nourishing and repairing, in talking to strangers, in caring for the other, essential to our existence. 100,000 years ago our species was not alone on Earth; there were at least four or five other human species. According to some anthropologists and evolutionary biologists such as Brian Hare, Homo sapiens prevailed not because it was the strongest and most physically fit species, but because it was the friendliest. This trait made possible survival, coexistence and cooperation, the sharing of knowledge, care for others, and development. Traditional working methods have reduced water to the idea of stock, a resource channelled for use, instead of a source of life present everywhere, in multiple states. These approaches tend to separate, to reduce the world to binary relations of a Cartesian matrix that thinks of reality mechanically. Within this framework, water appears predominantly as a liquid surface, imprisoned for human uses.

The current situation, resulting from an over-exploitation of resources, from non-redistributed economic growth and from demographic increase, combined with a context marked by profound ecological and socio-economic changes, imposes the need to develop sustainable relationships with planetary resources. We have to look for other kinds of relationships. Instead of separating and imprisoning, we must design an ecological and symbiotic rapprochement between humans, and between humans and non-humans, that includes the various spectra of reality, and water in its various forms and places. Work around water reveals to us a permeable mesh of interdependencies that connects us to the planet as a whole.

Dynamic Landscapes: Dancing margins, borders that don't exist

The lines that in cartography delimit the margins and distinguish land from water exist only on maps. The limits between these two masses are fluctuating, undefined and unstable; their exact delimitation is an impossibility. The restless character of water, the cyclical and constant movement of the tides pushed by the gravitational forces of the Earth, the Sun and the Moon, the porosity between land and water, make the margins zones of contact and mixing, places of interpenetration, and not places of division.

If we walk through an intertidal zone, the limits are constantly changing. The liquid tide covers and uncovers, but between the coming and going it seeps in, soaks into the wet sand, the stones and the beings of the ecosystems of these zones. If we imagine the bank of a river, the water circulates in a spiral, depositing and eroding its edge; it is an infinite process of transformation. Water penetrates into the land, first liquid, then in the form of humidity. We do not know when it becomes muddy, then clay and, finally, earth.

The territory of Porto and Matosinhos, the cities that host the Porto Design Biennale, was sculpted by rain, by rivers and streams (surface and underground) and by the sea. That is why this is also a proposal for a poetic, aesthetic, political, social, physical and affective relationship with the territory and the landscape, with its surface and underground bodies of water, with the potential of the natural resources of the tidal zones.

At a time when the rise of the mean sea level is accelerating and the margins, in some places, are going to change significantly, it is important to rethink what and how we should design, respecting and taking advantage of these ephemeral, dynamic and porous water lines. The margin can also be that border where we meet what is foreign to us, the stranger, the excluded, and it is also here that the journey, hope, can begin. The bank of a river or of the sea, places of departure and arrival, a place to reach, a place of encouragement, the beginning of a better life. Today the journey is often made because the water that existed and is needed for subsistence agriculture has become scarce, has disappeared. It is a journey that follows the water ‘imprisoned’ in the so-called more developed economies.

Flying Rivers: Rethinking the representations of water

The natural water cycle as it is usually presented offers a rather simplistic and limited explanation of how water circulates around the planet, summing up the world's hydrology as a linear process. Liquid water on the surface of the earth evaporates, clouds form, which break up and fall as rain or snow, becoming a liquid mass again in rivers, lakes, oceans... These generic representations leave out relevant aspects of how water moves, aspects that have to do with the regulation of the climate, the retention and circulation of water in nature in aqueous, plant, animal and other bodies, and its capture and processing for human uses. The perception that there is less and less water has become widespread. However, the amount of water on earth is thought to have remained stable for thousands of years, although population growth and the increase in the number of people replicating Western consumption patterns have led to increased retention of water for human consumption, in water storage and management systems such as dams and water transfers, to generate energy, for industrial production and for food. Many of these activities are based on a polluting extractive economy, which contributes to the deregulation of the climate and of water flows on earth and to the increased retention of water outside natural systems. The production of livestock, cereals, ceramics, textiles, furniture, machines, building materials and many other products causes enormous flows of water on a global scale, hidden flows, a kind of invisible flying rivers that circulate through trade. By way of example, and because it is never too much to remember, the transaction of a T-shirt implies the virtual trade of 2,700 litres of water, while a kilo of beef implies the use of 15,415 litres. In order to reveal other dimensions of the circulation of water, it is important to reflect on broad and inclusive strategies to represent and communicate not only these hidden flows and their relevance in the global economy of inequality, but also water as an omnipresent and vital relational flow, thereby developing a broader awareness of current water movements and uses of water.

Affective Geologies: The living history of a recipe

The taste of cherries also has the taste of water, of the water of the place where they grew. Flowers, plants, fruits and all foods are containers of water from the place where they were planted and harvested. Cherries are 82.6% moisture. An orange is only 10.4% orange; the rest is water. We often hear of people or places because of unique foods, meals and flavours, and of the impossibility of reproducing those flavours elsewhere when we use the same recipe. Water is one of the many factors that alter flavours. All waters have a different taste resulting from the mineral composition absorbed in the depths of the earth. These are geological flavours, brought from the bowels of the earth to the surface by water. To what extent do the availability and taste of the water used in growing and cooking food influence and nourish the flavours and the cuisine of a territory? How do they influence rituals, culture, memories, the construction of identity, the sense of belonging, the affective ties between people, and between people and places? This speculation on the social life of the taste of water may activate new imaginaries and knowledge, opening another perspective on the geographies of food, and on our relationship with the taste of water and of food. However, many other questions must be raised from the interaction between water, place, people and food. Critical questions that we must look at carefully, such as food insecurity and water scarcity, over-exploitation and environmental degradation, biodiversity and cultural diversity, social inequality and the consequent access to basic health care, climate change and its relationship with the exploitation of working conditions. The Latin expressions Cum (with) + panis (bread) are at the root of the words “company” and “companion”. A companion is, therefore, the one with whom we share bread, with whom we eat at the same table (Tolentino Mendonça, 2011). We must seek and tell stories that help us reflect on an ethics of production, distribution and consumption of food and water, that ‘hydrate’ creativity, responsible and fair consumption, equity and sharing, commensality and the building of affections, the sense of community.
