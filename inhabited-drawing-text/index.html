Inhabited Drawing (text)

Title Desenho Habitado (Inhabited Drawing); Year 2007; Category Text; Published in Prémio Massimo Dutti Design, Lisbon: Grupo Regojo, 2007; Fernando Brízio – Desenho Habitado, Lisbon: experimentadesign, 2011

A long time ago I saw a scene from a Buster Keaton film that remained latent in my memory and that, some years later, made me go looking for it. I found it in The High Sign. In this film BK picks up a can of paint and a brush, goes up to a wall, draws a coat hook and then hangs up his hat. When I saw this I recognised myself in what had just happened in front of me; it seemed that Keaton was personifying a designer.

For some of his films he conceived magnificent scenic devices in which objects, houses, systems and mechanisms surprise through their ingenuity and humour, taking on a central role in the unfolding of the story: the swapped numbering of the boxes containing a prefabricated house makes him, in the film One Week (1920), build a house whose walls, windows and doors end up crooked/oblique; the final result looks, in formal terms, like a deconstructivist building. In The Scarecrow the set is concentrated in a small room that holds bedroom, dining room and kitchen, which makes BK adopt various strategies for operating and using the objects, so as to make habitability and the performance of multiple domestic actions possible in a limited space. These objects transform; some are multifunctional, others are built into the walls, they create passages, fold over one another, are suspended from the walls and ceiling, are thrown, fly.

But let us return to the initial scene. In the rapid prototyping (a drawing that materialises instantly) that BK performs before us in The High Sign, we can see a partial synthesis of the design process. Which, although reductive because it leaves out many important questions in design, was for me a powerful stimulus to resume some reflection on the relations between drawing and design begun in 2003 in the project sound system.

In The High Sign we see a drawing turning into a material object that is used. In design, drawing is an unavoidable instrument; it serves multiple purposes: it helps us understand reality, interpret project contexts (human, economic, technological, productive), think, test and feel solutions, communicate results and build material or immaterial objects. The importance and effectiveness of drawing in design does not lie in the intrinsic qualities of the drawing inscribed on the sheet, that is, in the drawing as object, but in what happened while it was made (questions, answers, thoughts, sensations, physical and emotional relationship) and in what can be done with it afterwards (the drawing discovered/contains a solution/path of work, the drawing changed my being, it contributes to my knowledge and sensibility...). Drawing in design is not only operative in relation to a given subject; it is also the builder/shaper of the being who conceives and who experiences the world through it.

If we look around us we can see that everything around us that is not natural resulted from a process of conception and construction that at some point used drawing. We can say that the drawing that was at the origin of a large part of our artificial world remains latent in the objects, systems and spaces that surround us. We grab it, touch it, we are inside it. We inhabit a drawing.

That is why I say that designers are choreographers of our everyday life; with the help of drawing they conceive our habitat, a relational structure formed by objects, spaces, systems and services that works as a score for the movements, gestures and actions we perform every day. Over the course of our lives these drawings condition our everyday life invisibly; they slowly shape our body, our mind and the way we relate to the world. The work of design is also a work of design of man.
